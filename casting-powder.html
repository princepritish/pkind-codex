Casting Powder (Continuous Casting Mould Flux)

Low-consumption mould flux engineered for stable slag film formation, clean mould lubrication and controlled horizontal heat transfer.

Casting powder (also called continuous casting mould flux) is a synthetic mineral blend added to the surface of molten steel in a continuous casting mould. It melts into a liquid slag layer that flows into the gap between the solidifying shell and the mould wall, where it performs four jobs at once: lubricating the shell, controlling horizontal heat transfer, insulating the meniscus, and absorbing non-metallic inclusions rising out of the steel.

How the slag film forms

Casting powder is fed onto the meniscus as a free-flowing powder or granule. Heat from the steel drives three distinct layers: an unreacted powder layer on top that insulates, a sintered intermediate layer, and a fully molten liquid slag pool sitting directly on the steel surface.

Mould oscillation pumps that liquid slag into the mould–shell gap, forming a film typically 0.1–0.2 mm thick. The film has to stay liquid enough to lubricate but viscous enough not to drain away. Getting that balance right for a given casting speed is the whole design problem.

Selecting a grade by casting speed

Casting speed is the dominant selection variable. As speed rises, the residence time in the mould falls, so the powder must melt faster and the slag must have lower viscosity to keep feeding the gap. Under-melting at high speed is a common cause of longitudinal cracking and sticker breakouts.

Basicity controls crystallisation. Higher basicity slags crystallise more readily into cuspidine, which raises thermal resistance and slows heat extraction — useful for crack-sensitive peritectic grades. Lower basicity, glassier slags transfer heat faster and suit high-speed billet casting where lubrication dominates.

Carbon content controls the melting rate. Carbon particles physically separate the mineral grains and delay agglomeration, so raising carbon slows melting and thickens the insulating layer. This is the primary lever for tuning melt rate without changing base chemistry.

Common problems and what they usually indicate

Sticker breakouts generally point to insufficient liquid slag in the gap — either the powder is melting too slowly for the casting speed, or consumption is too low.

Longitudinal surface cracking on peritectic grades usually means heat extraction is too fast and too uneven; a higher-basicity, more crystalline slag moderates it.

Slag carryover into the strand and dark rims on billets typically indicate excessive slag pool depth or unstable meniscus behaviour.

Powder blackouts — bare patches where the steel surface is exposed — mean the feed rate or spreading behaviour is not keeping up with consumption.

FAQs

Casting Powder — frequently asked questions

What is casting powder used for?

Casting powder is used in continuous casting moulds to lubricate the gap between the solidifying steel shell and the mould wall, control horizontal heat transfer, insulate the meniscus against heat loss, and absorb non-metallic inclusions from the steel surface.

What is the difference between casting powder and mould flux?

They are the same material. 'Casting powder' and 'mould powder' are the common shop-floor terms in India, while 'mould flux' is more usual in academic and European literature. All three describe the mineral blend fed onto the meniscus in a continuous casting mould.

How much casting powder is consumed per tonne of steel?

Typical consumption is 0.30 to 0.60 kg per tonne of steel, though the actual figure depends on mould size, casting speed, oscillation practice and section format. Billet casting generally runs at the higher end per tonne because of the greater surface-area-to-volume ratio.

Can you supply casting powder for a specific casting speed?

Yes. Melting rate, viscosity and basicity are adjusted for low, medium and high casting speeds. Higher speeds require faster-melting, lower-viscosity grades to maintain a continuous slag film in the mould-shell gap.

Do you supply trial quantities?

Yes. Trial quantities are supplied with technical usage guidance, and the formulation is revised based on measured performance during the trial heats.
