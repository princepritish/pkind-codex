Nozzle Filling Compound

Free-opening compound engineered to resist sintering at ladle temperatures, so the slide gate opens on demand without oxygen lancing.

Ladle slide gate Free opening Low sintering Controlled PSD

Nozzle filling compound (NFC) is a granular refractory material packed into the ladle nozzle before tapping. Its job is to hold back the molten steel while the ladle is filled and held, then flow out freely under gravity when the slide gate is opened — giving what the industry calls a free open. If the compound sinters into a solid plug, the crew has to lance the nozzle with oxygen, which costs time, damages the well block and puts operators at risk.

Why free opening rate matters

Free opening rate is the percentage of heats where the ladle opens under gravity alone, without oxygen lancing. It is one of the cleanest single indicators of ladle practice health, and it has direct cost consequences: every lanced heat adds turnaround time, consumes oxygen, erodes the well block and nozzle, and introduces a safety exposure.

A well-matched compound in a stable practice typically achieves 90–98% free opening. Where the rate is consistently lower, the cause is usually one of four things: compound chemistry unsuited to the holding temperature, wrong particle size distribution, moisture pickup during storage, or filling practice that leaves voids or over-compacts the nozzle.

Material design: the sintering trade-off

The central design tension is that the compound must be refractory enough not to fuse, but must not form a rigid sintered bridge either. Chromite-based compounds are the workhorse because chromite has high refractoriness and a low tendency to react with ladle slag and steel at typical holding temperatures.

Silica sand is commonly blended in to tune bulk density, flow behaviour and cost. Carbon additions can be used to further reduce wetting and sintering, at the cost of some carbon pickup risk for ultra-low-carbon grades.

Particle size distribution is as important as chemistry. Too fine and the compound packs densely, conducts heat well into the bed and sinters. Too coarse and steel can infiltrate the voids and freeze, which produces exactly the plug the compound is meant to prevent. The controlled 0.10–1.50 mm band balances the two.

Filling and storage practice

The nozzle should be dry and clear of previous residue before filling. The compound is poured to fill the well completely, without ramming — compaction increases thermal conduction into the bed and raises sintering risk.

Moisture is the most common avoidable failure. The compound is supplied at 0.30% maximum moisture, but hygroscopic pickup in humid storage will undo that. Bags should be stored sealed, off the floor and under cover, and opened bags should be used within the shift.

Long holding times and high superheat both raise the sintering risk. Where a shop routinely holds heats for extended periods, we adjust the blend toward higher refractoriness rather than expecting a standard grade to absorb the difference.

FAQs

Nozzle Filling Compound — frequently asked questions

What is nozzle filling compound used for?

Nozzle filling compound is packed into the ladle nozzle before tapping. It holds back the molten steel during filling and holding, then flows out under gravity when the slide gate opens, allowing the ladle to open without oxygen lancing.

What is a good free opening rate?

A well-matched compound in a stable ladle practice typically achieves 90 to 98 percent free opening. Rates consistently below that usually indicate a chemistry mismatch to holding temperature, incorrect particle size distribution, moisture pickup in storage, or filling practice problems.

What is nozzle filling compound made of?

Most compounds are chromite based, often blended with silica sand to tune bulk density, flow and cost. Chromite is used because it has high refractoriness and low reactivity with ladle slag and steel at typical holding temperatures. Carbon may be added to further reduce wetting.

Why does a ladle fail to open freely?

The four most common causes are compound chemistry unsuited to the holding temperature, particle size distribution that is too fine (which promotes sintering) or too coarse (which allows steel infiltration), moisture pickup during storage, and filling practice that rams the compound or leaves voids.

Can the compound be tuned for our ladle size and holding time?

Yes. Composition and particle size distribution are adjusted for heat size, nozzle bore configuration, holding time, superheat practice and steel grade behaviour.
