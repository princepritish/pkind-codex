Ladle Covering Compound (Radex)

Low-density insulating cover that spreads fast across the ladle surface to cut radiative heat loss and shield the melt from reoxidation.

Temperature retention Reoxidation barrier Fast spreading 4x–8x expansion

Ladle covering compound — widely known by the trade name Radex — is a low-density insulating powder spread over the molten steel surface in a ladle. On contact with the melt it expands into a light, porous blanket that does two things: it suppresses radiative heat loss from the exposed steel surface, and it forms a physical barrier that limits contact between the steel and the atmosphere, reducing reoxidation and nitrogen pickup.

Where the heat actually goes

An uncovered ladle loses heat by radiation from the free steel surface, by conduction into the refractory lining, and through the ladle shell. The radiative component from the open surface is the largest single term in most operations, and it scales with the fourth power of absolute temperature — which is why it dominates at steelmaking temperatures and why covering the surface is so effective.

Cutting surface radiation lets the shop tap at a lower superheat for the same casting temperature. That has a compounding benefit: lower tap temperature means less refractory wear, lower energy consumption per heat, and better as-cast structure because a lower superheat at the mould promotes equiaxed solidification.

Dosage and application practice

Dosage of 0.80–1.50 kg per tonne covers most ladle sizes and holding practices. The correct figure for a given shop is the one that produces complete, continuous surface coverage — under-dosing leaves radiating gaps that undo most of the benefit, and over-dosing simply adds cost without proportionate gain.

Apply immediately after tapping, once the ladle has been filled. Applying too early risks the compound being disturbed by the incoming stream; applying late wastes the highest-temperature part of the holding period, which is exactly when radiative loss is greatest.

The compound is designed for rapid spreading so that a single addition covers the surface without manual raking. Where a ladle has a long holding time before casting, a topping-up addition part-way through holding can restore coverage disturbed by argon stirring.

Interaction with stirring and slag practice

Argon stirring will locally break the cover at the eye. This is unavoidable and generally acceptable for short stirring periods, but sustained hard stirring will disperse the layer and both the insulation and the reoxidation barrier are lost while the eye is open.

Ladle covering compound is not a substitute for a properly conditioned synthetic slag. The two do different jobs: the slag handles desulphurisation and inclusion absorption chemically, while the covering compound handles thermal and atmospheric protection physically. They are used together.

Ash content is held below 5% to limit the residue contributed to the ladle slag. High-ash covering products can shift slag chemistry unpredictably over repeated heats.

FAQs

Ladle Covering Compound — frequently asked questions

What is ladle covering compound used for?

Ladle covering compound is spread over the molten steel surface in a ladle to reduce radiative heat loss and to form a physical barrier that limits contact between the steel and the atmosphere, reducing reoxidation and nitrogen pickup during holding and casting.

What is Radex?

Radex is the widely used trade name for ladle covering compound, a low-density insulating powder that expands into a porous blanket over the molten steel surface. The terms ladle covering compound, Radex and ladle insulating powder are used interchangeably in Indian steel plants.

How much ladle covering compound is needed per tonne?

Typical dosage is 0.80 to 1.50 kg per tonne of steel. The correct figure for a given shop is whatever produces complete continuous surface coverage; under-dosing leaves radiating gaps that remove most of the benefit.

How much heat loss does a ladle covering compound prevent?

Typical reduction is 25 to 40 percent, depending on ladle practice, holding time and ambient conditions. The benefit comes mainly from suppressing radiation from the open steel surface, which is the largest single heat loss term in most ladle operations.

Does it replace synthetic ladle slag?

No. The two do different jobs and are used together. Synthetic slag handles desulphurisation and inclusion absorption chemically, while covering compound provides thermal insulation and atmospheric protection physically.
